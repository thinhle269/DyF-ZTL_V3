Reviewer 01:
Recommendation: Major changes
Dear Authors, the revised manuscript is materially stronger than the earlier version. You have responded constructively by adding multi-seed experiments, paired statistical testing, controlled implementations of robust aggregation methods, clean and attack-condition ablations, hyperparameter sensitivity analysis, full-run timing, and a more transparent limitations discussion. The revised interpretation of the clean-data results is appropriately restrained: DyF-ZTL significantly outperforms FedAvg but is statistically comparable to FedProx, while FLTrust remains competitive and performs better at several extreme poisoning ratios. These improvements are substantial, and the combination of an interpretable client model with persistent server-side update admission is relevant to secure federated systems. Nevertheless, the manuscript is not ready for acceptance because several issues affect the correctness of the central comparisons, the scope of the security conclusions, the external validity of the experiments, and the paper’s alignment with the Journal of Network and Systems Management.
The most important issue is an inconsistency between the federated aggregation described in the manuscript and the aggregation implemented in the public code. Equation 1 defines FedAvg using sample-size weights, . The released implementation instead computes an unweighted arithmetic mean of the client state dictionaries, and that same routine is used for the FedAvg and FedProx baselines and for the admitted DyF-ZTL clients. This distinction is material because the training records are distributed through a Dirichlet partition and then independently resampled with SMOTE on each client, producing potentially unequal effective client dataset sizes. Therefore, the reported FedAvg and FedProx experiments are not canonical sample-weighted implementations. You should either explicitly rename and justify the current methods as uniform client averaging or, preferably, implement canonical sample-size-weighted FedAvg and FedProx and rerun all affected clean-data, robustness, architecture-controlled, ablation, sensitivity, and timing experiments. For DyF-ZTL, both uniform admitted-client averaging and sample-size-weighted admitted-client averaging should be reported as an aggregation ablation. The manuscript should also provide the client sample counts before and after SMOTE so that readers can assess the practical magnitude of this issue.
The privacy claim also requires correction. The title describes the approach as “Privacy-Preserving,” yet the manuscript repeatedly and correctly acknowledges that the framework provides only training-data locality and does not protect model updates through differential privacy, secure aggregation, homomorphic encryption, or another formal privacy mechanism. Retaining “Privacy-Preserving” in the title is consequently stronger than the evidence supports. Unless an actual privacy mechanism and corresponding privacy–utility and inference-attack evaluation are added, the title, abstract, contributions, and conclusion should use wording such as “data-local,” “federated,” or “privacy-conscious” rather than “privacy-preserving.” Similarly, “Zero Trust-inspired update admission” is more precise than presenting the system as a complete Zero Trust Architecture. The manuscript should explicitly map the trust engine to the Policy Decision Point, Policy Enforcement Point, policy information sources, client identity lifecycle, revocation, rehabilitation, and audit functions, and it should distinguish low validation performance from evidence of malicious intent.
The evidence for robustness remains too narrow for the broader poisoning claims. The evaluated adversary performs a fixed cyclic label flip on its complete local dataset, trains normally, does not scale or replace model updates, does not coordinate with other attackers, and does not know the validation set, gates, or adaptive threshold. Participation is synchronous and complete in every round. Moreover, the poisoning sweep uses only three seeds and twenty communication rounds per configuration, despite the clean-data study using ten seeds and one hundred rounds. The reported performance at 80–90% compromised clients should therefore be described specifically as resilience to the evaluated static label-flipping data-poisoning attack, not as general model-poisoning or Byzantine robustness. The robustness experiments should be repeated over at least five, and preferably ten, matched seeds using a longer or convergence-justified training horizon. Confidence intervals or standard deviations should be shown for every method and poisoning ratio, rather than only selected methods. The evaluation should also include at least one targeted label-flipping attack, one backdoor attack, one model-replacement or update-scaling attack, and one adaptive or validation-aware attack. Partial participation and changing attacker populations should be tested because persistent identity-based trust may behave differently when clients participate intermittently.
The trust engine’s dependence on trusted server data and fixed absolute accuracy gates also needs deeper evaluation. The server holds 5,280 labeled validation records by default, representing a substantial clean corpus against which every submitted client model is evaluated. The current trusted-set-size analysis is useful but is confined to one poisoning ratio, three seeds, twenty rounds, and an in-distribution trusted set. The method should be evaluated when the trusted corpus is class-imbalanced, smaller, distribution-shifted, mildly contaminated, or missing classes that appear on individual clients. Gate calibration should be repeated under those conditions. This issue is particularly important because the hard admission rule excludes approximately 19–23% of honest updates at poisoning ratios 0.4–0.5, and the representative trajectory ends with only 12–13 of the 16 honest clients contributing. You should report malicious-update rejection, honest-update rejection, precision and recall of the admission decision, average admitted-client count, time to exclusion, and time to rehabilitation for every poisoning ratio and seed. A comparison between hard exclusion, trust-weighted aggregation, and a cooldown or probationary rehabilitation mechanism would directly quantify the integrity–participation trade-off.
The claims regarding non-IID mitigation and dynamic adaptation are not yet adequately demonstrated. The source records are randomly divided at row level into stratified training, validation, and test partitions, after dropping the timestamp fields, and synthetic clients are then constructed with a Dirichlet partition. These clients are not demonstrated to correspond to separate physical endpoints, users, sessions, or time periods. Consequently, statements about learning distinct user profiles and adapting to evolving endpoint behaviour exceed the experimental evidence. Where metadata permits, the evaluation should use host-, session-, or time-disjoint splitting; otherwise, the manuscript should acknowledge that correlated telemetry records may occur across the random splits. A chronological or source-group-disjoint experiment and, preferably, validation on a second endpoint-security dataset are needed. Because local SMOTE can substantially alter the original client distributions, you should report client class histograms and heterogeneity measures before and after SMOTE and include a no-SMOTE or class-weighted-loss ablation. The Dirichlet concentration should also be varied across several levels, and at least one additional FL method explicitly designed for heterogeneous data should be included. Without such experiments, the claim that the fuzzy architecture mitigates non-IID heterogeneity remains largely inferential.
The “dynamic” and “interpretable” aspects of the fuzzy component require stronger evidence. The manuscript explains how the Gaussian centres and widths are trained, but the operational examples are qualitative; it explicitly states that the stated “High Disk Write AND Low Network” interpretation is not a formally extracted symbolic rule and that the developer-workload example is illustrative rather than a logged observation. The clean ablation also does not establish a statistically significant independent benefit from either component. You should present actual learned membership functions, rule-firing distributions, cross-client variation, and stability across seeds, together with extracted or approximated rules for representative classes. A temporal or concept-drift experiment is necessary to substantiate “dynamic adaptation”; otherwise, “trainable neuro-fuzzy” would be the more defensible terminology. The implementation should also explain how valid fuzzy widths are maintained, since the released model learns unconstrained width parameters, and should assess numerical stability because the rule activation is the product of fifteen membership values.
The paper also needs a more explicit network and systems management contribution. The journal covers security and machine-learning technologies, but it expressly requires manuscripts to address management issues and solutions rather than merely applying an algorithm to a network-related dataset. At present, the study is predominantly a federated intrusion-classification and robust-aggregation simulation. The evaluation does not measure communication volume, client inference latency, peak memory, energy use, asynchronous participation, or behaviour in a live endpoint deployment. To establish journal fit, the revised manuscript should frame DyF-ZTL as a management-plane mechanism and evaluate its policy lifecycle, update-admission overhead, scalability with the number of clients and trusted-set size, communication and control-plane costs, client onboarding and revocation, failure handling, partial participation, and service impact. An emulated or small real deployment involving distinct endpoint processes would considerably strengthen the operational contribution. Without this management emphasis, the manuscript remains vulnerable to an out-of-scope decision even if the classification results are technically sound.
Several reproducibility and reporting issues should also be corrected. The manuscript specifies a three-round grace period, whereas the released trust-engine constructor uses warmup_rounds=2 and updates its round counter only after logging. This off-by-one convention happens to produce three observed warm-up rounds in the default run, but it makes the parameter semantics inconsistent with Algorithm 2 and complicates interpretation of the “four-round” sensitivity setting. The current round index should be passed explicitly to the trust-update function, the parameter should denote the exact number of grace rounds, and the affected sensitivity experiment should be rerun. The repository also describes the default main command as reproducing ten seeds, while the experiment driver defaults to five seeds; the reproduction instructions and executable defaults must agree. In addition, the implementation evaluates the test set after every communication round. The manuscript should confirm that these observations were never used for checkpoint, hyperparameter, or method selection; preferably, convergence monitoring should use validation data and the test set should be evaluated only at the predetermined final round.
Finally, the class-wise conclusions should be aggregated across seeds. Figure 6 and Table 4 use a single seed that the text acknowledges represents a weaker FedAvg run. A single illustrative confusion matrix may remain, but the main evidence should report per-class recall, precision, F1, and variability across all ten clean-data seeds, with a predefined and neutral rule for selecting any representative run. The revised submission should also include a clearly labelled Data Availability Statement identifying the dataset, code version, raw run records, and analysis artifacts. This is required for original research articles by the journal’s current submission guidelines. An archived, immutable release with a version identifier or DOI would be preferable to relying solely on a mutable repository branch.
Reviewer 2
This paper proposes DyF-ZTL, a federated learning framework for Zero Trust endpoint security. The framework contains two main components. First, a Dynamic Neuro-Fuzzy Network (DFNN) is deployed on each client, with Gaussian membership functions learned via gradient descent to handle heterogeneous Windows telemetry. Second, an Adaptive Trust Engine is deployed at the server to evaluate client models using a trusted validation dataset. Based on validation accuracy and historical trust scores, the server decides whether each client update should be admitted or excluded from aggregation. Here are some suggestions.
1. Both neuro-fuzzy learning and validation-based trust mechanisms have been investigated previously in federated learning. The main novelty appears to come from combining a trainable fuzzy classifier with a validation-based client admission mechanism. The authors should explain more precisely what new algorithmic principle is introduced beyond this integration. In particular, the Trust Engine mainly consists of manually designed accuracy gates, multiplicative penalties, a grace period, and an adaptive threshold.
2. More references related to dynamic learning, such as CuIoT: Advancing network connectivity with motif knowledge-centric for robust topology, Lego-motif: Enhancing IOT topology robustness with evolutionary motif-based generation, Fast robustness enhancement for dynamic IIoT topology with adaptive Bayesian learning.
3. The experiments consider only static, non-colluding label flipping. Attackers do not know the trusted validation dataset, accuracy gates, or trust threshold. This setting is relatively favorable for a defense whose decision is directly based on validation accuracy.
